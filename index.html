The problem

Most thrillers get the West Philippine Sea and South China Sea wrong.

Readers who follow the region's real tension keep running into the same three gaps in the fiction written about it.

01

Written from the outside

Most gray-zone fiction sits at the level of admirals and national command authority. You never meet the fisherman, the seafarer, or the sailor actually standing in the water.

02

Frozen in one headline

Most treat the region as a single flashpoint between two superpowers — not three or more rival networks working the same chokepoints at once, each with different appetites.

03

All strategy, no people

The human cost — hostages, families, remittance economies — gets reduced to a footnote instead of the engine that actually drives the plot forward.

The solution

Crosscurrent was built to fix that.

One retired captain. Three rival networks. Four real chokepoints. A series designed around the gray zone as it actually works.

1

Real geography

The story moves through four actual flashpoints — Scarborough Shoal, the Strait of Hormuz, the Gulf of Aden, and Diego Garcia — not invented stand-ins.

2

Three rival networks

Red Coral, Saffron Tide, and Razor Gyre — three intelligence and mercenary networks racing for the same stolen technology, each playing a different game.

3

Human stakes

A kidnapped Filipino seafarer and the daughter waiting for him keep the geopolitics tethered to the people who actually pay for it.

4

A five-book arc

Crosscurrent is Book I of a five-book series tracking one operating architecture as it moves from ocean to ocean.

Exactly as it appears in the book — the storm, the ghost ship, and the mission that starts everything.

The sea east of Luzon had gone black in the way only typhoon water could, less a color than an intention. From the open side door of the Navy helicopter, Lucas "Kal" Calderon watched the storm-torn Pacific pitch and heave below him in long, muscular swells. Rotor wash flattened the rain into silver slashes. Beyond the noise and darkness, lightning stitched brief white wounds across the horizon, revealing the ocean in fragments before hiding it again.

Then the ship appeared.

Noel B. Escalona — Captain, Philippine Navy · PMA "Masikhay" Class of 1999

Born and raised in Dumaguete City, Captain Escalona is a graduate of Silliman University and the Philippine Military Academy. A Command-at-Sea Badge holder and experienced ship captain, he has commanded Philippine Navy warships throughout the archipelago and served extensively in and around the West Philippine Sea and South China Sea, as well as on an overseas posting with the Combined Maritime Forces in the Kingdom of Bahrain — the same waters at the center of Crosscurrent. He holds a Master of National Security Administration from the National Defense College of the Philippines, a Master in Development Management from the Asian Institute of Management, and a Master in Military Command from the Naval Command College in Nanjing, PRC. Crosscurrent draws directly from his professional background and experiences at sea, blending operational realism with geopolitical fiction. He currently lives in Talisay City, Cebu with his wife, Christy Liza, and their children, Kristina and Nylah.
